The rule of thumb. Randomization balances nuisance variables on average, over many participants. Counterbalancing balances them by construction, every time. With a class-sized sample you cannot afford to wait for the average.

What the loop actually does

Your conditions file has 10 rows. The loop repeats it. These four settings decide the order you get — and one of them quietly breaks the balance you built into the file.

Each bar is one trial. Its height is a value taken from that row of the conditions file — stimulus duration, difficulty, whatever your design varies. Dashed lines separate repetitions.

How many times each row was used

Sampling without replacement. Think of the rows as chess pieces in a box: you shake it and draw them out one at a time, and a piece you have drawn does not go back in. That is what random does — every row appears exactly once per repetition, so any balancing you built into the file survives. Full random puts the pieces back, and the balance is gone.

Two different problems. A confound means something you did not intend is lined up with your conditions, so the result has more than one explanation. Low power means the design is clean but too small or too short to see the effect. The first one you fix by changing the design; the second by adding trials or participants. Do not treat every problem as a confound.
