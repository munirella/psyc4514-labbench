Why each part exists

  1. Fixation gives the eye somewhere to rest and signals a trial is starting.
  2. Stimulus is the thing you manipulate — here, a coloured word.
  3. Response is recorded with its timestamp.
  4. Blank keeps one trial from bleeding into the next.
Notice the black screen. Experiment displays are deliberately plain: no logos, no borders, nothing moving. Anything extra is a variable you did not intend to add.

Run a Stroop task on yourself

Name the ink colour and ignore the word. You will get your own reaction times back at the end, in the same shape as the file PsychoPy produces.

Before you start

Four colours, four keys. Keep your fingers on the keys the whole time. Go as fast as you can while staying accurate. There are four practice trials first.

On a phone or tablet, tap the buttons instead of using the keyboard.

One honest caveat. A browser cannot time as precisely as PsychoPy, which synchronises with the monitor's refresh. Your numbers here are good enough to see the effect, not to publish. That gap is the reason we use dedicated software from Week 3.

Why so many trials?

A single reaction time is mostly noise. Set a true effect, then see what your experiment would have reported — once, and then twenty times over.

Each dot is one whole experiment. The orange line is the true effect; the grey line is zero.

What to take away. With few trials, the same true effect can come back as anything from strongly negative to double its real size. Nothing about the mind changed between those runs — only the sample. This is what you are buying when you run 100 trials instead of 10.
